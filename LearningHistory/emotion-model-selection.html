Learning History / Emotion Model Selection

Choosing a Korean Emotion Model

The deep learning study behind Chicky's emotion classifier: how a data-leakage bug changed the whole comparison, and why KLUE-RoBERTa with focal loss became the final model.

#nlp #klue-roberta #focal-loss #data-leakage #pytorch

The task

Chicky needed to read a child's dialogue and label each sentence with one of seven emotions: fear, surprise, angry, sadness, neutral, happiness, and disgust. Those sentence labels are aggregated into a dominant emotion and a distribution, which feed the parenting report and a bedtime book theme. So the model had to be reliable per sentence, not just on average.

The bug that mattered more than the model

My first runs looked great, and that was the warning sign. The dataset contained duplicate utterances, and a naive random split let the same sentence land in both train and test. The model was partly memorizing, so the reported score was inflated by leakage rather than earned by generalization.

Fix: I switched to a group-aware split where every copy of an utterance is forced onto one side only, then re-ran every experiment on that leak-free split. From that point, all numbers describe unseen sentences.

This was the most useful lesson of the project: an honest split changed the ranking and the absolute scores more than any hyperparameter I touched. I now check for group leakage before trusting any first result.

Comparing three Korean models

On the leak-free split I benchmarked three pretrained Korean encoders under identical settings (max_len=64, batch=64, lr=5e-5, 5 epochs, fixed seed). KLUE-RoBERTa led on every metric.

The initial plan (from the proposal) had been KoBERT, but the honest comparison made KLUE-RoBERTa the clear pick.

Handling class imbalance with focal loss

The seven emotions were not evenly represented, so I trained with focal loss plus class weighting to stop the majority classes from dominating. I also ran a class-weighted cross-entropy variant to check the choice: KLUE-RoBERTa stayed first under both losses (0.9354 focal vs 0.9340 class-weight), a gap small enough to be noise. I shipped the focal-loss model.

Where it still struggles

Per-emotion F1 was lowest on angry (0.905). Looking at errors, the ceiling comes from genuine semantic overlap between angry, disgust, and sadness in short child utterances, not from the model architecture.

Serving

The final classifier is served behind a Flask app the backend calls: /predict returns the emotion scores for a batch of sentences, and /report adds the dominant emotion, distribution, and book theme used by the app. The trained weights ship with the service so it can serve immediately.

What I took away

  • Validate the split before celebrating a score. Group-aware splitting is non-negotiable when a dataset has duplicates.
  • A fair, identical-settings comparison is worth more than tuning one favored model.
  • Report per-class metrics; an average can hide the class that actually matters.
